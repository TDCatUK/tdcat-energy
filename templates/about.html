The Project

This dashboard is a passion project built and maintained by Torsten Dettlaff. It was created to bridge the gap between a personal home energy system and the realities of the Great Britain electricity grid and the markets behind it.

Why did I build it? Because I wanted to understand more about energy data and the infrastructure and markets behind it.

Everything is collected every 5 minutes, and the charts show the last 24 hours. Grid figures cover Great Britain (England, Scotland and Wales); Northern Ireland runs on a separate all-island grid.

Local Weather & Analytics

Weather data and 7-day forecasts are fetched via the Open-Meteo API. Site analytics are pulled from the Cloudflare GraphQL API.

  • Daylight Length & Irradiance: Alongside daylight length, the forecast charts track Direct Normal Irradiance (W/m²): the strength of direct sunlight on a surface facing the sun. It's a good guide to sunny spells and so to home solar output, though it leaves out the diffuse light panels still collect on cloudy days.
  • Temp, Wind, & Rain: Core weather data that helps explain grid behaviour, such as cold days driving up heating demand and windy days driving up wind generation.
  • Site Visitors (Header): The small user icon in the top navigation bar shows the number of visits to this dashboard over the last 24 hours, as counted by Cloudflare.

Home Metrics (Tesla Powerwall)

Read directly from the Powerwall gateway on the local network every 5 minutes, without going through Tesla's cloud.

  • Home Load: The electrical draw of the whole house at that moment, including everything left on standby.
  • Solar Generating (AC vs DC Est): The main figure is the AC (Alternating Current) power coming out of the inverter, available to the house, battery or grid. The smaller DC Est figure estimates the DC (Direct Current) power the panels produced before conversion, using the inverter efficiency set on the admin page. The difference is the energy lost as heat in the inverter and cabling.
  • Battery Level: The Powerwall's state of charge, shown the same way as the Tesla app. The app keeps a 5% reserve hidden, so the gateway's raw reading is rescaled: app % = (raw − 5) ÷ 0.95. The text underneath shows whether it's charging or discharging, and how fast.
  • Grid Flow: Power flowing between the house and the grid. "Importing" means buying from the grid to run the house or charge the battery; "Exporting" means selling spare solar or battery power back.
  • Today's kWh: Energy is power multiplied by time, so each "Today" total adds up the area under the 5-minute readings since midnight UK time (the trapezoidal rule: the average of each pair of readings multiplied by the time between them).

Octopus Energy

Rates and meter readings come from the Octopus Energy API. Smart meter readings reach Octopus through the DCC (Data Communications Company) network, usually the next day.

  • Live Import Rate: The price (in pence per kWh, including VAT) for electricity imported during the current half-hour on the Agile tariff, which changes every 30 minutes.
  • Live Export Rate: The price paid (in pence per kWh) for electricity exported during the current half-hour on Agile Outgoing.
  • Elec Import/Export (Meter kWh): The billable energy recorded by the smart meter for one whole day (midnight to midnight, UK time), shown with its date. It's the latest day for which the import, export and gas meters have all reported. Export and gas readings tend to arrive a day after import, so this is often two days ago.
  • Gas Used (Meter kWh): Gas meters measure volume in cubic metres (m³), but gas is billed in kWh. The dashboard converts it with the standard formula: m³ × 1.02264 (volume correction) × calorific value (about 39.5 MJ/m³) ÷ 3.6, which comes to about 11.2 kWh per m³. Your bill uses the actual calorific value for the period, so it may differ slightly.

National Grid Metrics

Grid data comes from Elexon's BMRS (Balancing Mechanism Reporting Service), the NESO data portal, NESO's Carbon Intensity API and PV_Live from Sheffield Solar at the University of Sheffield.

  • Grid Demand: Total electricity demand in Great Britain, in gigawatts (GW). This is the "Actual Demand Gross" figure explained below: everything the transmission grid supplies plus the estimated output of rooftop solar and small local wind.
  • Generation: Total output of every metered power station, plus estimates for solar and small embedded wind. Pumped storage only counts while it's generating; when it's pumping, that's demand, not generation.
  • Net Flow: The net flow through the interconnector cables linking Great Britain to France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, Ireland and Northern Ireland. A down arrow means GB is importing overall; an up arrow means it's exporting.
  • Frequency (Hz): Mains frequency rises when there's more generation than demand and falls when there's less, so it shows the balance on the grid second by second. NESO must keep it between 49.5 and 50.5 Hz by law and normally keeps it within 49.8–50.2 Hz. The card shows the latest reading from Elexon; the chart shows the reading at each 5-minute snapshot.
  • Carbon Intensity (gCO₂/kWh): Grams of carbon dioxide emitted for each kilowatt-hour of electricity generated in GB, for the current half-hour. It uses the measured figure when one has been published, otherwise the forecast. It falls as wind, solar and nuclear make up more of the mix, and rises with gas.

Wholesale Markets & Imbalance

Electricity is traded in half-hour blocks. Suppliers and generators trade up to an hour before each half-hour; after that, NESO balances the grid in real time and the cost of balancing is charged back to whoever was out of balance.

  • Market Index Price (MIDP): The volume-weighted average price of short-term trades for the latest half-hour on the EPEX SPOT GB market, as published by Elexon. It's a good guide to what electricity is trading for on the wholesale market right now. Half-hours with no trades are skipped rather than shown as £0.
  • System Imbalance (NIV): The Net Imbalance Volume for the latest settled half-hour, in MWh. A positive (short) figure means there was less energy than needed, so NESO had to buy more by asking generators to increase output or users to cut demand. A negative (long) figure means there was too much, so NESO paid to reduce generation or increase demand.
  • Balancing Price (System Price): The imbalance price for the latest settled half-hour. Anyone who generated or used a different amount from what they contracted pays or receives this price for the difference. Since 2015 there has been a single price, so the System Sell Price and System Buy Price are the same. It's set by the cost of the actions NESO took to balance the grid, so it can spike far above, or drop far below, the market price.

The 4 Levels of Demand

Elexon and NESO measure demand in a few different ways, depending on what's included. The breakdown table and chart on the dashboard show all four.

1. National Demand (INDO)

The demand that transmission-connected power stations and imports have to meet for GB consumers, including homes and businesses connected through local distribution networks. It leaves out exports, pumped-storage pumping and power stations' own use. Rooftop solar and small local wind reduce it, because the grid only sees the demand they don't cover. The dashboard works this out from ITSDO, and it matches Elexon's own INDO figure to within about 1%.

2. Transmission System Demand (ITSDO)

Everything the transmission grid has to supply. It is National Demand plus:

  • Exports: Electricity sent abroad through the interconnectors.
  • Pumped Storage Pumping: Power used to pump water uphill into reservoirs (like Dinorwig) so it can be released through turbines later.
  • Station Load: Electricity power stations use to run themselves. Elexon and NESO count this as a fixed 500 MW.

3. Actual Demand Net

An estimate of what GB homes and businesses are actually using. It's National Demand plus Embedded Generation: rooftop solar and smaller local wind turbines (LV Wind). These sit on local distribution networks rather than the transmission grid, so the grid can't see them; it just sees National Demand drop when the sun comes out or the wind picks up. This figure adds Sheffield Solar's and NESO's estimates of that output back in.

4. Actual Demand Gross

The broadest measure: Transmission System Demand (including exports, pumping and station load) plus embedded solar and wind. This is the figure on the Grid Demand card and the dashed line on the generation chart.

Generation Fuel Types & The Dashed Line

The stacked colours on the generation chart show where electricity is coming from: every fuel type plus imports. The dashed line shows demand.

What is the White Dashed Line?

The dashed line is Grid Demand (Actual Demand Gross). Supply and demand on the grid balance at every moment, so the stack and the line track each other closely, usually within a few percent. Any surplus is used straight away by exports, pumped storage and batteries, which are counted as demand. The small gaps you can see come from how the data is collected, not from real over- or under-supply: generation is a 5-minute snapshot, demand is a half-hourly average published after each half-hour, and solar and embedded wind are estimates.

  • HV Wind: Output from large onshore and offshore wind farms connected to the transmission grid. Often GB's biggest single source of electricity.
  • LV Wind (Embedded): Estimated output from smaller wind turbines connected to local distribution networks. The grid can't measure these directly, so the figure is NESO's estimate for the current half-hour.
  • Solar: Sheffield Solar's PV_Live estimate of national solar output, scaled up from thousands of monitored solar installations.
  • Nuclear: Steady, low-carbon output from GB's nuclear power stations, which can't easily speed up or slow down.
  • Gas (CCGT): Combined Cycle Gas Turbines: efficient gas power stations that use the hot exhaust from a gas turbine to drive a steam turbine as well.
  • Gas (OCG): Open Cycle Gas Turbines: quick-starting but less efficient gas plants, mainly used for short peaks or when the grid is tight.
  • Hydro & Pumped Storage: Hydro is output from rivers and reservoirs. Pumped storage releases stored water through turbines when it's needed. When it's pumping water back uphill, that's counted as demand, not as negative generation.
  • Biomass: Power stations (like Drax) that burn wood pellets and other organic material. Officially classed as renewable, though that's debated.
  • Other: Generation Elexon doesn't put under a main fuel type, such as energy-from-waste and some smaller plants. The dashboard also adds coal and oil here; GB's last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Imports: Electricity flowing into GB through the interconnectors. Interconnectors exporting at the same time are counted as demand.

Grid Batteries (Unofficial Estimate)

There's no official live figure for grid-scale batteries yet, so this dashboard estimates one from Balancing Mechanism data.

  • How it's worked out: Every unit in the Balancing Mechanism tells the grid what it plans to do each minute (its Physical Notification). When NESO tells a unit to do something different (a Bid-Offer Acceptance), that instruction takes over. Every 5 minutes the dashboard adds up the current level of each battery unit, using the latest instruction where there is one and the unit's own plan otherwise.
  • Which units count: Elexon doesn't have a "battery" fuel type, so batteries are picked out of the Balancing Mechanism unit register by National Grid's naming convention (a "B" as the fifth character of the unit ID, e.g. BLWNB-1) or by names such as "Battery", "BESS" and "Energy Storage". That currently finds about 150 units with roughly 7 GW of capacity.
  • Why it's unofficial: These are planned and instructed levels, not metered output, and batteries outside the Balancing Mechanism aren't included. The real figure can be somewhat different, so treat it as a good indication rather than an exact measurement.

National Gas Data

From the National Gas Transmission data portal. The live flows are 2-minute readings published every 12 minutes; the dashboard collects them every 5 minutes. Gas flows are in million cubic metres per day (mcm/d): one mcm of gas holds roughly 11 GWh of energy.

  • Linepack: The gas held in the pipes of the National Transmission System, in million cubic metres (mcm). It rises when more gas is coming in than going out and falls when less is, and that buffer covers the swings in demand through the day.
  • Supply, and where it's coming from: Gas entering the national system right now. North Sea (UK & Norway) is the pipelines landing at St Fergus, Teesside, Bacton and elsewhere; Norway (Langeled) is the direct pipeline into Easington; LNG is shipped gas from the Isle of Grain and Milford Haven terminals; Storage is gas being withdrawn from storage sites; Continent is gas coming in through the BBL and IUK pipelines from the Netherlands and Belgium.
  • Demand, and where it's going: Gas leaving the national system right now. Homes & businesses is gas passed to the local distribution networks; Power stations and Industry are large users connected directly; Exports go to Belgium, the Netherlands and Ireland; Storage injection is gas being put into storage.
  • Storage (% full): Gas in GB storage sites compared with their total capacity, from the latest complete gas day (the gas industry's day runs from 5am to 5am). LNG terminal tanks are shown separately.
  • Gas for power (approximate): Gas burned by power stations, converted to energy using a typical calorific value (39.5 MJ per m³), compared with the electricity gas plants are generating at the same time. It gives a rough idea of how much of the gas's energy ends up as electricity. It's approximate because the real calorific value varies and some of that gas goes to combined heat and power plants.
